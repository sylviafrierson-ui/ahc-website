Desk Work and Your Neck: Small Breaks That Add Up

By Dr. Sylvia Frierson, DC · October 30, 2026

Most of us spend a big part of the day looking at a screen. If your neck feels tight by mid-afternoon, you are far from alone. Here is why desk work asks so much of your neck, and a few simple habits that many people find helpful to try.

Education only. This article is general information, not a diagnosis or medical advice. Sudden severe pain, numbness or weakness, trouble speaking, vision changes, or neck pain after a fall or accident needs emergency care right away.

Why a desk day is hard on the neck

Your head weighs about 10 to 12 pounds when it sits balanced over your shoulders. When it drifts forward to read a screen, the muscles in the back of your neck and shoulders work harder to hold it up.

Muscles are built to move. Holding one position for hours, even a comfortable-feeling one, can leave them tired and tight. That steady effort is a big reason a long desk day can feel so heavy at the neck.

A quick setup check

You do not need fancy equipment. A few small adjustments can make a difference in how your day feels.

  1. Screen height. The top of your screen should be about eye level, so you are not looking down. A laptop riser or a stack of books works well.
  2. Screen distance. About an arm’s length away. If you find yourself leaning in, the text may be too small.
  3. Feet and hips. Feet flat on the floor, hips back in the chair, and knees roughly level with your hips.
  4. Elbows and wrists. Elbows close to your sides and bent near a right angle, with wrists fairly straight.

Using a laptop? A separate keyboard and a riser let you set the screen and your hands where each one needs to be.

Your best posture is your next posture

No one position is perfect for eight hours. The goal is to change positions often. Try setting a gentle reminder every 30 to 45 minutes to stand, walk to the water cooler, or simply shift how you are sitting.

If you take calls, stand up for them. If you can, alternate between sitting and standing during the day.

Three easy breaks to try

These are gentle and take under a minute. Move slowly and stop if anything feels sharp or wrong.

  • Chin glide. Sit tall and slide your chin straight back, as if making a gentle double chin. Hold a couple of seconds, then relax.
  • Shoulder rolls. Roll your shoulders up, back and down a few times.
  • Look far away. Every so often, rest your eyes on something across the room or out a window. Your eyes and the small muscles at the base of your skull both get a break.

What your body may be telling you

Occasional tightness after a long day is common. It is worth paying attention if you notice tension that keeps returning, headaches that seem to start at the base of the skull, or a head that tilts to one side. These are things to mention to your provider. They are clues to notice, not a diagnosis.
