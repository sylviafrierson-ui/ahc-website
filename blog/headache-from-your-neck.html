Is Your Headache Coming From Your Neck? The Anatomy Explained

By Dr. Sylvia Frierson, DC · October 30, 2026

If you have ever had a headache that seems to start at the base of your skull and creep up over your head toward your eyes, the anatomy behind it is worth knowing. Some headaches actually start in the neck. Here is how the top of your neck and your head are wired together, explained in plain language.

Education only. This article is general information, not a diagnosis or medical advice. A headache that is sudden and severe, or comes with fever, a stiff neck, confusion, weakness, trouble speaking or vision changes, or follows a hit to the head, needs emergency care right away.

Where your skull meets your spine

The top bone of your neck is the atlas, or C1. It is a ring of bone, and your whole skull balances on it, about the weight of a bowling ball. Just below it is C2, the axis, which lets you turn your head side to side.

These two bones are the most mobile in your entire spine. Bones do not hold themselves together; soft tissue does, meaning ligaments and tendons. At the top of the neck, that soft tissue is the smallest in the neck, which is why this area is so vulnerable after falls, sports injuries and car accidents.

Why your neck and your head share the same wiring

The nerves from the top three levels of your neck (C1, C2 and C3) carry sensation from the back of your head and upper neck. In the brainstem, those nerves meet at the same relay station as the nerve that carries sensation from your face and forehead.

So your brain receives signals from the upper neck and the head at the same switchboard. That is why irritation in the upper neck can be felt as a headache, even behind the eyes. There is a medical name for headaches that come from the neck: cervicogenic headache, which literally means “starting in the neck.”

The tiny muscles under your skull

Right under the base of your skull are four small muscles on each side, called the suboccipitals. They are packed with position sensors because they help your head and eyes make tiny, precise movements. Even reading a line of text keeps them busy.

Now picture your head pushed forward over a laptop or a phone for hours. Those little muscles work overtime the whole time, which is the tight, ropey feeling so many people notice at the base of the skull.

Three things to notice about your headaches

If you are wondering whether your headache is coming from your neck, these are worth noticing and sharing with your provider. They are clues, not a diagnosis.

  1. Where does it start? A neck-related headache often starts at the base of the skull or upper neck and spreads forward, and it is often on one side.
  2. Does neck position change it? Notice whether it shows up after long desk days, long drives or a certain sleeping position.
  3. Is your neck stiff when it happens? In a mirror, slowly turn your head right, then left. Is one side tighter? When you look straight ahead, is your head tilted?
